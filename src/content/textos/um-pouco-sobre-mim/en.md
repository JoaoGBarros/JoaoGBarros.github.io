My interest in this field started in the most cliché and simple way possible, and I'm sure you've heard someone in tech tell a similar story. The reason? Video games.

Since I was a kid I've been fascinated, obsessed really, by games, whether card games, make-believe games or, of course, video games. I remember my sixth birthday like it was yesterday, when my mom gave me the present that would change my life, my first console, a Game Boy Advance with a ~~bootleg~~ Pokémon Emerald cartridge. Nobody knew it then, but that was the starting point of my interest in computing and of a passion that lasts to this day (for Pokémon too, which is still my favorite franchise).

Years went by, and the curiosity that started with the Game Boy spread to other things. At school, my interest in science only grew. I used to say I wanted to be a scientist, and I spent hours watching documentaries. That's where another passion came from, astronomy. I read book after book (for kids, of course) about the Solar System, about all the worlds that exist beyond ours, and about how ridiculously interesting the universe we live in is. That pulled me toward a subject I was never good at, math, which slowly won me over throughout school.

At the same time, like many kids and teenagers in the 2000s and 2010s, I was growing up alongside the internet and the computer, and I couldn't contain my excitement whenever the classic "computer class" came around. That's where I learned the basics of using a computer and, from then on, I became the official "IT guy" at home. With the computer came, of course, the games of that era, like Grand Chase, Ragnarok and World of Warcraft. But none caught me like a game that was still in its early days, one nobody imagined would become one of the biggest names in the industry: Minecraft.

At first, I spent months playing alone, until I discovered multiplayer. GAME CHANGER. I logged into servers like Craftlandia, a big Brazilian server, every single day (eternal nostalgia for Craftlandia on version 1.7.3), until an idea came up: "what if I made a server to play with my friends?".

Building that server was my first contact with computer networks. After all, how were my friends supposed to join my server? While looking for answers (without ever imagining I'd one day work with exactly this), I went through the classics, like Hamachi, and through the question that haunted a whole generation of server owners: "what the \*\*\*\* does opening a port on the router even mean?". After a lot of research (or rather, more YouTube videos than I'd like to admit), it finally worked!

Two years later, it was thanks to Minecraft that I met André, who became a great friend through middle school and high school. And he was with me on my first contact with programming, when we had a brilliant idea: "let's make a mod?".

It was 2012, the same year I started playing a little game that was getting popular, something called LoL. Minecraft mods were simpler back then, and there weren't nearly as many tools, engines and tutorials as there are today. With what little we had, we started "learning" Java, quotes intended, because we basically copied what was in the video and tweaked it for our needs, without understanding the language or the basic concepts behind it. We made textures for the blocks and played with something we had created ourselves. It was just a new ore, a new pickaxe and a new sword, exactly what the tutorial taught, but it was ours. Shortly after, we abandoned the project, and my next contact with programming would only come in 2018.

While programming was on pause, the dreams kept changing. The kid who wanted to be an astronomer suddenly wanted to be a biologist, a chemist, a chemical engineer... maybe... a historian? There were so many options, but there was one constant, an interest in new technologies. Understanding what happened behind the screen and building something of my own with it was what excited me most.

In the end, that constant won. In 2016, in my second year of high school, I realized my calling was computing. In 2018 I started Computer Science at Universidade Vila Velha with one dream in mind: "I want to make a game". That's where I came back to programming, and this time for real, no copying tutorials. In the first semester I learned C, what variables were, their types, loops... It was the first step toward that game. But the more I learned, the more another wish grew, to live the experience of a federal university. So I made a tough call and put my enrollment on hold in the second semester. I studied to get into the Federal University of Espírito Santo (UFES), and got in. In 2019 I started Computer Engineering, after a little push from someone I knew (that change of course will probably get an article of its own).

When I was choosing what to study, I kept reading that computing was a huge field, but nothing prepared me to see it in practice. At UFES, that dream of making a game started sharing space with other interests, like artificial intelligence, computer security, computer networks (hello again, router port) and, the biggest of them all, software engineering.

That's where I found myself. To me, programming was never just about making the code work. It's about understanding the problem of whoever is going to use it, deciding how the pieces fit together, choosing what can stay simple and what has to be robust, and making sure the system keeps running when things go wrong. In the end, software engineering is building software with people, deadlines and everything that happens after the first "it works!" in mind. I lived that at LabES, building systems used by the Espírito Santo State Secretariat of Culture, and I live it today as a backend engineer.

Today, in 2026, I'm in my last semester (about time, right? But let's cut me some slack, there was a pandemic in between). And that dream of making a game? It never went away, it just got some company.

This site is where I'll tell the rest of that story. There will be projects and the decisions behind them, sure, but also more personal pieces, with reflections, experiences and whatever else crosses my mind along the way. Thanks for reading this far.
